Your Business Is Connected. Is It Protected?

The technology that makes running a local business easier can also create risks many owners may not think about every day.

Think about a typical Ashburn business today.

The internet may power the point-of-sale system. Employees may connect laptops, tablets, and phones to Wi-Fi. Security cameras may be accessible remotely. Customer information may be stored in cloud services. Email may be used for invoices, vendor communication, scheduling, and password resets.

All of these tools make running a business easier.

But they also mean that protecting a business is no longer only about locking the doors at night.

Start With the Basics

Business protection doesn't always require complicated technology. Some of the most important improvements begin with simple questions.

  • Is your guest Wi-Fi separated from the network used by business systems?
  • Is multi-factor authentication enabled on important accounts?
  • Does every employee have their own account instead of sharing passwords?
  • When someone leaves the company, is their access removed?
  • Are important files backed up somewhere other than the computer that created them?
  • Do you know who has administrator access to your systems?
  • Are your computers, routers, cameras, and other connected devices receiving security updates?

A business owner doesn't need to become a cybersecurity expert to start asking these questions.

Physical Security and Digital Security Are Becoming Connected

A security camera is a physical security device, but today it may also be connected to the internet.

The same can be true of door-access systems, thermostats, alarms, payment terminals, digital menus, and other equipment.

That means physical security and cybersecurity increasingly overlap.

Protecting the building matters.

Protecting the systems operating inside it matters too.

Know What Your Business Depends On

One useful exercise is surprisingly simple:

Ask yourself what would happen if your internet, email, payment system, or primary computer stopped working tomorrow.

Could you continue serving customers?

Could you accept payments?

Could you access your business records?

Could you recover your information?

Knowing those answers before something happens can make a major difference when something eventually goes wrong.
